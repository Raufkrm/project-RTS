This type of extreme, multi-scale simulation isn't about creating and storing every detail in advance. That would be impossible. Instead, it relies on two core, dynamic concepts:
Level of Detail (LOD) and Procedural Generation.
Here is an analogy to explain the level of detail required:
Imagine a mapmaker tasked with creating a detailed atlas of the entire world, but with an impossible constraint: every time you zoom in on a specific city block, the map must show every single fire hydrant and window pane. The mapmaker can't draw every detail for every scale at once.
Instead, a "dynamic mapmaker" would do this:

    From space: It generates a simple, low-detail map of the continents and oceans. You see a solid green shape for a forest because from far away, that's all you need to see.
    As you zoom closer: When you move toward a particular country, the mapmaker's tools create more detailed features, like rivers, mountains, and the borders of states. The solid green shape begins to resolve into distinct areas of forest and farmland.
    As you approach a city: The mapmaker swaps the landscape details for city details, generating roads, buildings, and parks on the fly. The game engine is "streaming" in new information that wasn't previously necessary.
    On the surface: When you land on the planet, the mapmaker uses a "seed" (a small amount of pre-defined data) and algorithms to procedurally generate the exact location. This is where your buildings, units, and resources for the RTS game would be created. The level designer doesn't hand-craft every rock; they create the rules for what kind of rocks appear there.

The key concepts in more detail
1. Level of Detail (LOD)
This is the process of displaying different, pre-made versions of an asset based on its distance from the camera.

    For the RTS: From space, a planetary city might be rendered as a single, low-polygon mesh with a simple texture.
    On the surface: When you zoom in, that simple shape is replaced by a complex, high-polygon model of the city, complete with individual buildings, lights, and windows.
    The illusion: The transition is managed smoothly so you never notice the swap. The game engine saves immense computing power by only rendering high-detail assets when they are close enough to be seen clearly.

2. Procedural Generation
This technique is the secret to generating limitless detail without a massive storage footprint. Instead of storing every tree, rock, and building, the game stores a single algorithm and a "seed" value.

    The Seed: A small number that acts as the blueprint for an entire planet. As long as the game engine uses the same seed and algorithm, it will always generate the same planet with the same features.
    The Algorithm: A set of rules that tells the game engine how to use the seed to "draw" the world. For an RTS game, this might include rules for:
        Terrain: Using noise patterns to create mountains, valleys, and rivers.
        Placement: Using rules to determine where resource deposits and other game elements should be placed.
        Unit Assets: Generating variations of buildings and units on the fly.

Example: Combining LOD and procedural generation
Imagine a space RTS like No Man's Sky or Star Citizen.

    You are in orbit: The planet you see is a low-polygon sphere. The game uses procedural generation to create the overall look, like cloud patterns and continental shapes.
    You fly down: The game begins to generate the terrain of the landing area using the planet's unique seed. It creates realistic features like mountains and craters using noise algorithms.
    You land and zoom in on your base: The game now loads in the high-detail models for your buildings and military units. These were generated procedurally based on their placement rules, and now the LOD system ensures you see the most detailed version of each asset.
